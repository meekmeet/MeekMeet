Blessed are the meek - audited as a participant, a shepherd applicant, a search engine and a
regulator would each see it.

Audit date: 21 November 2026 (Sydney time)
Domain: https://meekmeet.com
Method: Text-fetch crawl of every sitemap URL and every linked route, corrected by a live-browser crawl of the same
site on the same day (21 Nov 2026, 4:31-4:37pm AEDT). No account was created, no sign-in completed, no
application submitted, no mailbox tested.
Pages verified: 13 of 13 live - 12 in sitemap.xml plus /circles/global-new-moon, which is not in the sitemap.

    Verdict. MeekMeet is the warmest site in this audit series and, technically, one of the cleanest: all 13
    pages load, there are no dead links, the 404 page is a clean custom page, and the service is
    genuinely free. Its problem is not engineering. It is that the site asks for the most sensitive
    documents in the entire series - government photo ID, a Working With Children Check, a police
    check and a first aid certificate - while naming no legal operator anywhere: no entity, no ABN or
    ACN, no address, no named person. The Privacy Policy's own collection list then omits the photo ID
    entirely. Layered under that are a promise of "anonymised" summaries that the Privacy Policy itself
    contradicts, a Qur'an verse labelled as the Psalms, and a circles page that lists a circle and reports
    "No circles found" on the same screen.

Prices, counts and quotations in this report are taken verbatim from the live site on 21 November 2026.
Where the text crawl and the live browser disagreed, the live browser governs and the disagreement is
disclosed in the corrections addendum overleaf. Every finding is labelled with the method that established
it.

What Meek Meet is. A community listening platform built around a monthly rhythm: local circles, led by
verified "shepherds", gather at each new moon, answer gentle questions about community, housing,
environment and daily life, and turn the answers into reports intended for councils and decision-makers. It
begins from Matthew 5:5 - "Blessed are the meek, for they shall inherit the earth" - and explicitly sets its
table for every faith and none. It is Australia-first and, live, has exactly one circle: the Global New Moon
Circle, online, open to all faiths.

What is genuinely good. The child-safety page is the strongest safety document in this audit series: seven
sections, a zero-tolerance CSAE policy, named reporting channels in four countries, and a named contact,
safety@meekmeet.com. The shepherd bar itself - photo ID, Working With Children Check, police check
within 12 months, current first aid - is set high and stated without hedging: "No exceptions. No shortcuts."
Sign-in is passwordless, the account-deletion page gives real timelines (confirmation within 48 hours,
deletion within 30 days), and the /media page honestly labels what is live and what is still in development.

Where it fails. Every one of those strengths is undermined by a single absence: nobody is legally home.
The About page says "Meek Meet is not an organisation. It is a movement of gentle hearts." The Terms of
Service, in the same breath, take a licence over user content, reserve a right to terminate accounts, and
choose the courts of New South Wales - acts only an operator can perform - without ever naming one. A
shepherd applicant is asked to hand identity documents to that unnamed operator, and the Privacy Policy
that should explain the photo ID does not list it among the documents collected. The fixes are short and
cheap; they are ranked in the final chapter.

FINDING F-01 - P0

No legal operator is named anywhere

The Terms of Service run to 12 sections. They set eligibility, take a licence over submitted content (section
6), reserve termination rights (section 8), disclaim liability (section 9) and choose governing law: "These
Terms are governed by the laws of Australia. Any disputes will be resolved in the courts of New South
Wales" (section 11). The Privacy Policy commits "Meek Meet ('we', 'us', or 'our')" to protecting its
communities. Neither document - nor any other page on the site - ever says who "we" is.

    No company, trust, incorporated association or sole-trader name appears on any of the 13 pages [both].
    No ABN or ACN appears anywhere [both].
    No postal or street address appears anywhere; contact is by email only - privacy@meekmeet.com,
    legal@meekmeet.com and safety@meekmeet.com [text-fetch]. None of the three mailboxes was tested.
    No founder, director or responsible person is named anywhere [both].

Why this is the headline. Every other site in this series names somebody, even when the naming
contradicts itself. MeekMeet is the only one that contracts, disclaims and takes licences in the name of a
brand with no legal person behind it on the page. That matters most here because of what the site collects:
the next finding. A shepherd who uploads a passport-grade identity document, a police check and a
children's-worker clearance is entitled to know - on the page, before uploading - which legal person
receives it, under which privacy regime, and against whom a complaint could be made. At present the
answer, from the site's own documents, is: nobody who is named.

   The fix is one paragraph. Name the operator once, in the Terms and the Privacy Policy: legal name,
   ABN or ACN if any, a contact address, and the jurisdiction already chosen (New South Wales). Every
   other finding in this chapter gets easier the moment that paragraph exists.

FINDINGS F-02 AND F-03

The photo ID the Privacy Policy forgot, and the movement that signs
contracts

F-02 - P0: Government photo ID is collected but absent from the Privacy Policy [text-fetch]
The About page's Duty of Care section sets four mandatory verifications, and is explicit that all four are
required: "If a leader cannot provide all four verifications, they cannot lead a Meek Meet." The first is
identity:

   "Every leader submits government-issued photo ID. We confirm name, address, and identity before anyone is
   approved." - /about, Identity Verified

The other three - a Working With Children Check, a national police check issued within the last 12 months,
and a current first aid certificate (HLTAID011 or equivalent) - are all faithfully listed in the Privacy Policy's
collection section, which names "Working With Children Check, police check, and first aid documentation".
The photo ID is not there. Section 1 lists account information, community responses, circle information,
those three verification documents, and usage data - and stops. The single most sensitive document in the
set, the one that confirms "name, address, and identity", is the one the collection notice omits.

The Terms make the gap wider: section 5 has applicants "consent to verification of your identity and
safeguarding credentials" without naming the document either. An applicant reading the two legal
documents before applying would not learn that a government photo ID is required, how long it is kept, or
when it is destroyed. The /delete-account page does confirm that "your shepherd application and
verification documents, if any" are deleted on account deletion - the only retention statement that covers
the ID at all, and it sits on a page nobody links to (Finding F-08).

F-03 - P1: "Not an organisation" vs a contracting operator [text-fetch]

   "Meek Meet is not an organisation. It is a movement of gentle hearts." - /about, Who We Are

As positioning, the line is lovely. As law, the rest of the site disagrees with it. A movement cannot grant itself
a licence over user content, terminate accounts, verify police checks "directly", or submit to the courts of
New South Wales - an operator does those things, and the Terms repeatedly act as one. The contradiction
is not pedantry: the same documents that disclaim being an organisation are the documents a user, a
shepherd or a regulator would rely on when something goes wrong. Pick one account and make both
pages tell it.

FINDING F-04 - P1

"Anonymised" on the sales page, individual in the policy

The promise a prospective shepherd reads is consistent and repeated. The /shepherd page offers
"Beautiful, anonymised response summaries" and "Anonymised community reports". The Privacy Policy's
section 3 reinforces it: "Individual responses are never exposed publicly", and reports shared with "councils,
MPs, or other authorities contain only anonymised summaries". The Terms say shepherds work with
"anonymised, aggregated community insights".

Section 4 of the same Privacy Policy then says the opposite about the person who runs the circle:

   "Circle shepherds can see responses submitted within their circle." - Privacy Policy, section 4, Who Can See
   Your Data

That is individual, attributed visibility - a shepherd seeing what each member of a small local circle actually
wrote - not an anonymised summary. The Child Safety Standards confirm the same design from the other
direction: "Community responses within circles are visible to shepherds and administrators for
accountability." There are honest reasons for that design: safeguarding, moderation and accountability all
argue for it. What there is not is a way for both descriptions to be true at once.

Why it ranks P1. The people Meek Meet exists for - "the quiet, the overlooked, the vulnerable", in its own
words - are being invited to share concerns about housing, safety, money and spiritual well-being. A
participant who reads the marketing will reasonably believe their shepherd receives aggregates. A
participant who reads section 4 learns their shepherd reads their answers. In a circle of eight neighbours,
even a genuinely aggregated summary is often attributable in practice, which makes precision here more
important, not less.

   The fix is wording, not engineering. Say, on /shepherd and in section 3, what section 4 already
   says: your shepherd can see your responses within your circle; what leaves the circle - to councils,
   MPs and the public Voice dashboard - is anonymised and aggregated. That sentence costs nothing
   and converts a contradiction into a trust feature.

FINDING F-05 - P1

One promise, nine traditions - and two of the labels are wrong

The "One Promise, Many Names" table is the best idea on the site: nine traditions, each arriving at the
meek inheriting the earth in its own words, closing with "Different words. One promise. Every street." It
appears on both the homepage and /about. It is also where the site's fact-checking is weakest - on the
page whose entire purpose is accuracy across faiths.

The Qur'an verse labelled as the Psalms [text-fetch]

   "Islam - written in the Psalms: 'Indeed, My righteous servants shall inherit the earth.' - Qur'an 21:105"

The quotation and its citation are the Qur'an's. The label above it calls the entry "written in the Psalms".
Qur'an 21:105 itself refers back to what was written in the Psalms, which is presumably how the label went
wrong - but as published, the table files an Islamic scripture under a Jewish and Christian book, in the one
section of the site a Muslim visitor is most likely to read first. In a table built to honour every tradition's own
words, each entry's label has to be beyond argument. This one is not.

"All faiths and none" vs "We Unite" [text-fetch]
/media states the inclusion promise in its strongest form: "All Faiths and None - Christians, Muslims,
Buddhists, Hindus, Jews, Sikhs, secular humanists, the searching and the certain - every seat at the circle
is equal." The Global New Moon Circle repeats it: "people of all faiths, cultures and backgrounds... whoever
you are, wherever you are, you are welcome here."

The About page's own "We Unite" section defines the same unity very differently: "Catholic, Protestant,
Jehovah's Witness, Orthodox - all who hold the Bible dear find common ground in loving their neighbour."
Four Christian traditions, a Bible test, and nobody else. A Muslim, Jewish or secular reader who lands on
/about meets a narrower door than the one /media promised them. The homepage's framing compounds it:
nine traditions are quoted, but every structural section around the table - the tools, the rhythm, the call - is
carried by Christian scripture alone. That may be an honest reflection of the project's origins ("Christian is
the spark", as the site itself puts it, well). "We Unite" should then say what the spark page says, instead of
quietly redefining the table.

FINDINGS F-06 AND F-07

A circle that is there and not there, and reports that do not exist yet

F-06 - P1: /circles lists a circle and reports none [live]

Live, /circles does two contradictory things in one viewport sequence. It lists the site's one circle - the
Global New Moon Circle, online, all faiths - and directly below the card it renders the empty state: "No
circles found. Try adjusting your filters", with no search term entered and no filters applied. The text fetch,
which saw only the empty state, would have reported the page as simply empty; the live browser shows the
real defect, which is worse and stranger: the data is present, and the page denies it underneath. For a first-
time visitor this is the whole product - find a circle, join it - failing at step one while the evidence that it works
sits on the same screen. It is also, in fairness, almost certainly a small front-end state bug: the empty state
is not being suppressed when results render. Small cause, first-impression cost.

The circle's own page, /circles/global-new-moon [both], is welcoming but bare: no gathering dates or times,
no member count, no next-meet information - just the description, "Online - Zoom", and a sign-in prompt to
RSVP. The one concrete, joinable thing on the site gives a visitor no date to put in a calendar.

F-07 - P1: Reports presented as present tense; the pipeline says otherwise [both]

The homepage promises outcomes in the present tense: "Meet data becomes beautiful reports. Trends
emerge. Patterns speak." About step 06 completes the arc: "Reports reach councils, leaders, and decision-
makers. The meek do not shout - they prove." Against that, the live evidence: /voice, the public face of the
reporting engine, is an empty placeholder - "Global insights will appear once circles begin sharing their
voices" [live]. And /media, to its credit, labels its own pipeline honestly: live, steps 1 (Monthly Circle
Questions) and 2 (The Voice) are Live, while step 3 (Meek Meet Journal) and step 4 (Meek Meet Media)
are In Development [live].

So the overstatement is specific and fixable: it lives on the homepage and /about, not on /media. One circle
exists, no circle has yet shared a voice publicly, and no report output could be found anywhere on the site.
The honest labels already written for /media should be promoted to the two pages that currently skip them.

CHILD SAFETY AND ACCOUNT DELETION

The best safety page in the series - that nobody can find

The Child Safety Standards [text-fetch] deserve the praise first. Seven sections cover a zero-tolerance
policy on child sexual abuse and exploitation - naming CSAM, grooming and sexualised depictions of
minors including AI-generated imagery - age restrictions, prevention, in-app reporting, escalation, a contact
point and review. The escalation section names the National Center for Missing & Exploited Children, the
Australian Centre to Counter Child Exploitation, the eSafety Commissioner, the Internet Watch Foundation
and Israeli authorities. The prevention section states the platform design plainly: no open public chat
rooms, no stranger direct messaging, no anonymous photo sharing. The designated contact is
safety@meekmeet.com, and the page is dated - last updated 16 October 2026 [live].

Two tensions sit inside it. First, the age story: the platform is "not directed at children under 13" and is rated
13+, yet the standards describe a design in which "no adult can privately message a minor outside
supervised, recorded circle spaces" - which contemplates minors aged 13 to 17 participating in circles, in
person, under shepherds whose defining checks are children's-worker clearances. That is coherent, but no
page aimed at parents explains it in one place. Second, the app claims on this page - an in-app Contact
Admin reporting screen, recorded circle spaces - describe a mobile app that this web audit could not
inspect; they are recorded here as claims, not verified features.

F-08 - P1: Both safety pages are orphaned [live]

Neither /child-safety nor /delete-account is linked from any menu or footer on the site. Both were found only
through sitemap.xml, and both render as bare pages with no header or footer navigation - a reader who
lands on one has no onward path but the back button. These are the two pages a worried parent, a
safeguarding auditor or a platform reviewer (the child-safety page states it is published to meet Google
Play's policy requirements) is most likely to be sent to, and the two pages a user exercising a deletion right
most needs. The Privacy Policy does not link to the deletion page either, despite describing deletion as a
right. One footer link each closes this finding.

/delete-account itself is good [both]. It offers in-app deletion, an email route via
privacy@meekmeet.com, a 48-hour confirmation and a 30-day completion commitment [live], an itemised
list of what is deleted - including shepherd verification documents - and an honest statement of what is
kept: anonymised aggregates and legally required records. Finding F-14 is minor by comparison: the page
says in-app deletion removes data "immediately", while the Privacy Policy routes the same right through
contacting the operator; the two descriptions should be reconciled.

LOGIN, CODE OF LOVE AND AI

Good sign-in, one phantom document, one unexplained button

Sign-in is done properly [live]

Live, /login is passwordless: enter an email address and "we'll email you a secure link" - "No password
needed" - with "Continue with Google" as the alternative. For this audience, that is the right call: no
passwords to forget, reuse or leak, and nothing sensitive stored server-side beyond the account itself. The
text fetch showed only the email field and the consent line, which is a reminder of how much of this page is
script-rendered. The consent line itself reads: "By signing in, you agree to our Terms and Code of Love" -
which leads directly to the next finding.

F-10 - P2: The Code of Love does not exist [both]
The "Code of Love" is referenced at the moment of consent - the login page, and the footer link of the same
name [live]. Both point to /terms. The Terms of Service contain 12 sections - About, Eligibility, Account
Responsibilities, Community Conduct, Shepherd Verification, Content and Data, Reports, Termination,
Disclaimers, Changes, Governing Law, Contact - and no "Code of Love" heading or content of any kind
[text-fetch]. The live check confirms there is no separate Code of Love page [live]. So the one document a
new user is told they are agreeing to, alongside the Terms, at the exact moment they sign in, is a document
that does not exist. Either publish it - the site's tone suggests it would take an afternoon and be genuinely
on-brand - or remove the reference from the consent line and the footer. Agreeing to a phantom document
is worse than not mentioning one.

F-11 - P2: "Reviewer access" [live]

The live login page also displays a "Reviewer access" button. Nothing on the page labels, explains or
contextualises it. It was viewed and not clicked, and this report makes no claim about what it does or why it
is there. An unexplained privileged-looking control on a public sign-in page invites exactly the curiosity and
probing it presumably exists to serve quietly. Label it, gate it, or move it.

F-13 - P2: AI synthesis, disclosed but unbounded [text-fetch]
The Privacy Policy discloses that responses are processed by AI: "We use AI to synthesise themes,
sentiment, and consensus from aggregated community input", and names Groq among its processors
alongside Supabase, Resend and Google Cloud. Disclosure itself puts MeekMeet ahead of most sites in
this series. What is missing is any boundary around it: no opt-out, no statement of whether a human
reviews a synthesis before it goes to a council or an MP, and no retention statement for data sent to the AI
processor. For reflections that may touch faith, health, housing stress and safety, one sentence each on
review and retention would close it.

TECHNICAL AND SEO

Clean plumbing, lazy titles

What is right [both]. All 13 pages load. There are no dead links. The 404 is a clean custom page, not a
soft-404 or a stack trace [live]. /dashboard redirects to /login rather than erroring [live]. robots.txt allows
crawling, disallows /dashboard and /api, and declares the sitemap [text-fetch]. Most pages carry distinct,
descriptive titles: "Who We Are", "Find Your Circle", "Become a Shepherd", "Media & Mission", "Sign In",
"Privacy Policy" and "Child Safety Standards" [text-fetch]. The countdown is real and specific - Thursday 10
December 2026, 01:22 am AEDT [live] - and the shepherd application is correctly gated behind sign-in
rather than exposed as a public form collecting identity documents [text-fetch].

F-12 - P2: Four pages share the generic title [live]

/voice, /shepherd/apply, /circles/global-new-moon and the 404 page all carry the site's default title, "Meek
Meet | Blessed Are The Meek", instead of their own. Three of the four are pages a user is likely to share or
bookmark - the public insights page, the application gate and the one circle's page - and the 404's generic
title makes a broken external link harder to diagnose. Unique titles are a ten-minute fix with outsized search
and sharing value.

F-09 - P2: The sitemap omits the only circle [both]
